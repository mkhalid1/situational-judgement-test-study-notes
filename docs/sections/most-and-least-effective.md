# Most and least effective

**In one line:** you read a work scenario and pick the best response and the worst response from a short list.

This is the most common SJT format, and the UCAT ranking question is a three-option version of it. You make two judgements per item, so there are two ways to drop a mark, and the second pick (the worst option) is where most people lose them.

## What it measures

The format checks whether you can tell good judgement from poor judgement when every option sounds plausible. Employers want to see that you:

- deal with the real problem, not just the noise around it
- keep people safe and treat them fairly
- stay honest and follow the rules that apply to you
- know the limits of your role and bring in the right person
- act in good time and in proportion to the problem

The UCAT says its SJT looks at integrity, perspective taking, team involvement, resilience and adaptability. The UK Civil Service Judgement Test ties its scenarios to the Civil Service Behaviours. Different frameworks, same habits underneath.

## How the questions look

A short scenario, usually three to six sentences, tells you who you are, what has happened and any rule that matters. Then you see three to five possible actions. You choose one as **most effective** (or most appropriate) and one as **least effective** (or least appropriate).

In the UCAT version there are three options, and you pick the most and least appropriate. Elsewhere four or five options is normal. The method is the same.

## The scoring frame used in these notes

Real SJTs are keyed by panels of people who know the job well. You can't see their key, but their judgements follow a pattern. Every item in these notes is keyed against five checks:

1. **Problem:** does it deal with the actual problem?
2. **People:** does it keep people safe and treat them with respect?
3. **Rules:** is it honest, and does it follow the policy that applies?
4. **Role:** does it stay within your role, and bring in the right person when something is beyond you?
5. **Timing:** is it prompt and in proportion?

The **most effective** option passes all five. The **least effective** option fails the most important check in the scenario, usually safety, honesty or confidentiality, or makes the situation worse. An option that simply does nothing is bad, but an option that causes harm is worse.

## A method that works

1. **Read the scenario for three things:** your role, the risk, and any rule that is stated.
2. **Say the problem in one short sentence.** "A client is about to get wrong figures." "A resident may be being harmed."
3. **Score each option against the five checks.** A quick tick or cross in your head is enough.
4. **Pick the most effective first.** It is usually the one that fixes the problem *and* involves the right person.
5. **Then hunt for harm.** For the least effective, look for the option that breaks trust, breaks a rule, puts someone at risk or inflames the situation.
6. **Compare the two worst options directly.** Ask: which one leaves things in a worse state than doing nothing? That is your least effective.

## Traps that cost points

- **Picking "do nothing" as least effective by reflex.** Doing nothing is weak, but an option that breaches confidentiality or lies to someone is usually worse.
- **Rewarding heroics.** Going to someone's house, overriding a till, or treating a patient yourself can sound caring. If it is outside your role, it rarely scores as best.
- **Choosing the polite option over the complete one.** Being kind matters, but the best answer also fixes the problem.
- **Escalating too early or too late.** Going straight to a senior person before talking to a colleague is often second best. Waiting days when someone may be at risk is never best.
- **Using what you would really do on a bad day.** The key reflects good practice in the role, so answer as a capable person doing that job well.
- **Missing the stated rule.** If the scenario mentions a policy, it is there for a reason. The best answer usually follows it.

## Timing

The UCAT SJT has 69 questions in 26 minutes, which is a little under 23 seconds per question on average, and each scenario carries up to six questions. The Civil Service Judgement Test is not timed, and GOV.UK says most people take two to four minutes per scenario.

As guidance for practice: give yourself about 45 seconds for a four-option most-and-least item. Read the scenario once, carefully, and do not reread the options more than twice. If you are stuck between two "least" options after that, pick the one that causes harm rather than the one that only fails to help.

## Practice items

These 5 items are original, written for these notes. They are not taken from any employer's or publisher's test. For each item choose the **most effective** and the **least effective** option. Item 2 has three options, like the UCAT.

**Item 1.** You are a graduate analyst. Your manager asked you to send a client report by 5 p.m. At 3 p.m. you notice that a figure in the summary table does not match the source data in the appendix, and you think the summary is wrong. Your manager is in meetings until 4.30 p.m. but can be reached by message.

- A. Send the report at 5 p.m. as it is and say nothing. Your manager approved the draft yesterday.
- B. Message your manager now, explain the mismatch, and ask whether to correct it, delay or flag it to the client. Meanwhile check the source data again.
- C. Correct the summary figure yourself, send the report, and tell your manager afterwards.
- D. Ask a colleague on the team to double-check the figure before you decide what to do.

**Item 2.** You are a medical student on placement. You see a junior doctor about to examine a patient without cleaning their hands. The ward has a clear hand hygiene policy.

- A. Politely remind the doctor before they touch the patient, for example by offering the hand gel.
- B. Say nothing now, and report the doctor to the consultant at the end of the week.
- C. Say nothing. As a student, it is not your place.

**Item 3.** You work on a council's customer helpline. Your job is to log queries and transfer calls, and there is a procedure for welfare concerns. A caller says their elderly neighbour has not been seen for four days, post is piling up and the curtains have not moved.

- A. Tell the caller to ring back next week if they still have not seen the neighbour.
- B. Follow the welfare concern procedure: take the details and pass them to the right team urgently, and tell the caller to dial 999 if they think someone is in immediate danger.
- C. Go round to the address yourself after work to check.
- D. Log it as a general enquiry for the housing team, who reply within ten working days.

**Item 4.** You work in HR administration. Over lunch, a friend from another department asks whether a colleague of theirs is about to be made redundant. You have seen the list, and it is confidential.

- A. Tell them, as long as they promise to keep it to themselves.
- B. Say that you cannot discuss confidential HR matters, and change the subject.
- C. Hint that they "might want to be nice to" the colleague this week.
- D. Say that you do not know.

**Item 5.** You are a supervisor in a supermarket. A customer tells you there is spilt cooking oil across aisle 6. You are on your way to open a till because a queue is building.

- A. Go to the till. The spill can wait until the cleaner's next round.
- B. Ask a colleague nearby to stand by the spill and warn shoppers while you fetch the cleaning kit and a wet floor sign, then go to the till.
- C. Put a wet floor sign by the spill, go to the till, and send someone to clean it once the queue is down.
- D. Thank the customer and ask them to tell a cleaner if they see one.

<summary>Answers and explanations</summary>

**Item 1: Most B, least A.** B deals with the problem, keeps the decision with the person who owns the report, and uses the time left well. A knowingly sends wrong figures to a client, which harms the client and the firm's trust. *Why not C as most:* it may fix the figure, but you would be changing a signed-off client document without checking, and you might be the one who is wrong. *Why not D as least:* asking for a second check is cautious and slow, but it does no harm.

**Item 2: Most A, least C.** Patient safety comes first, and a polite reminder at the right moment protects the patient without embarrassing anyone. C leaves the patient at risk and ignores a clear policy. *Why not B as least:* it is late and does nothing for this patient, but it does at least raise the concern with someone senior.

**Item 3: Most B, least A.** The signs point to someone who may be seriously unwell, so speed matters. B follows the procedure, gets the concern to the right team and gives the caller a safe next step. A delays help by a week when every day could matter. *Why not C as most:* it is caring but outside your role, and you would be alone at an address with possibly urgent needs. *Why not D as least:* it is far too slow, but it does create a record, which is more than A does.

**Item 4: Most B, least A.** B protects confidential information and is honest about why you cannot answer. A is a direct breach of confidentiality, and a promise to keep it quiet does not change that. *Why not C as least:* a hint is also a breach and is poor, but A gives away the information in full. *Why not D as most:* it keeps the secret but is not true, and B does the same job honestly.

**Item 5: Most B, least A.** Spilt oil is a slipping hazard right now. B makes the area safe straight away and still gets the till covered. A leaves a known hazard in place on purpose. *Why not C as most:* a sign helps, but nobody is warning shoppers or cleaning it until the queue clears. *Why not D as least:* it passes the job to a customer, which is poor, but A makes a deliberate decision to leave the hazard.

## Score yourself

Give yourself one mark for each correct "most" and one for each correct "least", out of 10.

| Item | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 | 5 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Most | B | A | B | B | B |
| Least | A | C | A | A | A |

- **9 to 10:** your instincts match good practice. Move on to the rating formats, where the scale is finer.
- **7 to 8:** check whether your misses were mostly "most" or mostly "least". Most people lose marks on "least" by choosing "do nothing" over an option that causes harm. Reread step 6 of the method.
- **6 or fewer:** go back to the five checks and score every option out loud before you choose. Pay special attention to stated rules and to your role.
