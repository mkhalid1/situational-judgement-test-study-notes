# Rating effectiveness

**In one line:** you rate each possible action on its own, on a four-point scale from very good to very bad.

This is the format used in the second part of the UK Civil Service Judgement Test, and the UCAT uses the same idea when it asks how appropriate an action is. Because every action is judged on its own, two actions can get the same rating. That single fact changes how you should read the options.

## What it measures

Rating items check whether you can judge an action on its merits, without comparing it with the others. It is closer to real life than ranking: at work you rarely see four neat options side by side. You see one idea and decide whether it is a good one.

## How the questions look

A scenario, then a set of actions (the Civil Service test uses four actions per scenario). You give each action one rating. Two scales are common:

| Civil Service Judgement Test | UCAT appropriateness |
|---|---|
| Effective | A very appropriate thing to do |
| Fairly Effective | Appropriate, but not ideal |
| Ineffective | Inappropriate, but not awful |
| Counterproductive | A very inappropriate thing to do |

GOV.UK says to rate each action independently and that several actions can get the same rating. In the UCAT, rating questions are asked from a particular person's point of view, so read whose shoes you are in.

## The scoring frame used in these notes

Each rating in these notes is keyed against the five checks used throughout the guide:

1. **Problem:** does it deal with the actual problem?
2. **People:** does it keep people safe and treat them with respect?
3. **Rules:** is it honest, and does it follow the policy that applies?
4. **Role:** does it stay within your role, and bring in the right person when something is beyond you?
5. **Timing:** is it prompt and in proportion?

And this is how the checks map onto the four labels:

| Label | Meaning in these notes |
|---|---|
| **Effective** / **Very appropriate** | Passes all five checks. You would be glad a colleague did this. |
| **Fairly Effective** / **Appropriate, but not ideal** | Helps with the problem, but misses something: a step, a person who should know, or the best timing. |
| **Ineffective** / **Inappropriate, but not awful** | Does not solve the problem, or solves it badly, but does not cause serious harm. Doing nothing, or acting far too late, usually sits here. |
| **Counterproductive** / **Very inappropriate** | Makes things worse: harms or embarrasses someone, breaks a rule or trust, or creates a new problem. |

## A method that works

1. **Read the scenario and name the problem** in one sentence.
2. **Cover the other actions** in your mind. Judge each action as if it were the only one.
3. **Ask two questions in order.** First: *does it cause harm or break a rule?* If yes, it is the bottom label. Second: *does it help solve the problem?* If no, it is the second-lowest label.
4. **If it helps, ask: is anything missing?** If nothing important is missing, it is the top label. If it skips a person, a step or the best timing, it is the second label.
5. **Do not force a spread.** It is fine for two actions to share a rating. It is also fine for none to be the top label.

## Traps that cost points

- **Ranking instead of rating.** Giving the four actions four different labels because they feel like a set. The test does not ask for that.
- **Marking every imperfect action as bad.** "Fairly Effective" exists for actions that help but are not complete. Use it.
- **Treating "do nothing" as the worst.** Doing nothing usually means the problem stays the same. "Counterproductive" is for actions that make it worse.
- **Judging an action by its intention.** Kind intentions do not make an action effective if it breaks a rule or leaves the main problem untouched.
- **Ignoring whose point of view you have.** In UCAT-style items, rate what *that person* should do in their role, not what a senior doctor or manager would do.

## Timing

The Civil Service Judgement Test is not timed. GOV.UK says most people take two to four minutes per scenario and to allow at least an hour. In the UCAT, rating questions sit inside a section of 69 questions in 26 minutes, so each rating needs to take seconds, not minutes.

As guidance: once you have named the problem, each rating should take about 15 seconds. The two-question test in step 3 is what makes that possible.

## Practice items

These 5 items are original, written for these notes. Scenario 1 uses the Civil Service scale. Scenario 2 uses the UCAT appropriateness scale. Rate each action on its own.

### Scenario 1 (Civil Service scale)

> You are an administrative officer in a government agency. A member of the public emails you, upset, saying their application has been "lost". The system shows it was received and is in a queue that is currently six weeks long.

Rate each action: Effective, Fairly Effective, Ineffective or Counterproductive.

**Item 1.** Do not reply. They will get a decision when their turn comes.

**Item 2.** Reply promptly, confirm the application was received, explain the current waiting time honestly, and say how they will be kept updated.

**Item 3.** Move their application to the front of the queue so they stop worrying.

**Item 4.** Reply to confirm that the application was received, and nothing more.

<details>
<summary>Answers and explanations, items 1 to 4</summary>

**Item 1: Ineffective.** The person stays worried and may chase again, but nothing is made worse. *Why not Counterproductive:* silence does not create a new problem or break a rule.

**Item 2: Effective.** It answers the real worry (the application is safe), sets honest expectations and keeps the person informed. *Why not Fairly Effective:* nothing important is missing.

**Item 3: Counterproductive.** It is unfair to everyone who applied earlier and breaks the normal process. It also rewards complaints over need. *Why not Ineffective:* it actively causes a new problem.

**Item 4: Fairly Effective.** It deals with the main worry, so it helps. But they still do not know how long to wait or how they will hear, so they may write again. *Why not Effective:* it misses the timescale. *Why not Ineffective:* it does solve the core problem of thinking the application is lost.

</details>

### Scenario 2 (UCAT appropriateness scale)

> Sara is a medical student. She has been given some printed patient notes to read for a case write-up. At home that evening, she realises she has accidentally taken the printed notes home in her bag.

How appropriate are each of the following responses by Sara? Choose: A very appropriate thing to do, Appropriate but not ideal, Inappropriate but not awful, or A very inappropriate thing to do.

**Item 5.** Sara brings the notes back the next day and puts them back without telling anyone.

<details>
<summary>Answers and explanations, item 5</summary>

**Item 5: Inappropriate, but not awful.** The notes come back quickly, which limits the risk. But hiding a confidentiality slip fails the honesty check: nobody can confirm the notes were not seen, and nothing is learned. *Why not appropriate, but not ideal:* keeping a breach of patient confidentiality quiet is more than a small gap. *Why not very inappropriate:* the notes are returned the next day and she does not misuse them.

</details>

## Score yourself

| Item | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 | 5 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Answer | I | E | C | FE | IA |

Key: E = Effective, FE = Fairly Effective, I = Ineffective, C = Counterproductive. VA = A very appropriate thing to do, A = Appropriate but not ideal, IA = Inappropriate but not awful, VI = A very inappropriate thing to do.

- **5:** your sense of the scale is well calibrated.
- **4:** look for one-step misses. If you often put "do nothing" at the bottom, reread the second trap. If you often put helpful-but-incomplete actions at the top, reread step 4.
- **3 or fewer:** go back to the label table and the two-question test. Say the questions out loud for each action until they become habit.
