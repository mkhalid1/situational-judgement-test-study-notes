# Ranking responses

**In one line:** you put four possible responses to a work scenario in order, from most effective to least effective.

Ranking is the most demanding SJT format, because one item tests four judgements at once. Graduate schemes and employer SJTs use it often. The good news is that most of the marks come from getting the top and bottom right, and the same method that works for [most and least](most-and-least-effective.md) gets you there.

## What it measures

Ranking shows how finely you can grade judgement. Spotting the best and worst option is a start. Ranking also asks whether you can tell "helpful but incomplete" from "harmless but useless", which is the difference between a solid candidate and a strong one.

## How the questions look

A scenario sets out your role, what has happened and any rule that applies. Then four responses, A to D. You give each a rank from 1 (most effective) to 4 (least effective), using each rank once.

Some tests ask you to drag the options into order. Others give you a drop-down for each one. Some score partial credit when your order is close to the key: the UCAT, for example, says it gives full marks when a response matches the correct answer and partial marks when it is close. Check your own test's instructions for how ranking is scored.

## The scoring frame used in these notes

Every item is keyed against the same five checks used throughout these notes:

1. **Problem:** does it deal with the actual problem?
2. **People:** does it keep people safe and treat them with respect?
3. **Rules:** is it honest, and does it follow the policy that applies?
4. **Role:** does it stay within your role, and bring in the right person when something is beyond you?
5. **Timing:** is it prompt and in proportion?

In these items, the four ranks usually fall into four bands:

| Rank | Typical profile |
|---|---|
| 1 | Fixes the problem, in the right way, with the right people involved |
| 2 | Takes real action that helps, but is incomplete, late, or skips a step |
| 3 | Avoids the problem or only delays it, without directly causing harm |
| 4 | Causes harm: breaks a rule, deceives someone, or puts someone at risk |

Real tests are not always this neat, but the pattern holds far more often than not.

## A method that works

1. **Name the problem and your role** in one sentence each.
2. **Find rank 1.** Look for the option that passes all five checks.
3. **Find rank 4.** Look for harm, deception or a broken rule. Doing nothing is rarely rank 4 if another option actively makes things worse.
4. **Split the middle two.** Ask: does this option take real action on the problem? The one that does is rank 2. The one that avoids or postpones is rank 3.
5. **Sanity check.** Read your order from top to bottom. Each step down should be clearly worse than the one above. If two feel equal, look again at timing and at who is told.

## Traps that cost points

- **Ranking by how nice an option sounds.** "Offer to help" sounds warm, but if it does not touch the real problem it belongs in the middle.
- **Treating doing nothing as the worst.** Silence is weak. Lying, breaching confidentiality, or a physical response are usually worse.
- **Putting the solo fix above the team fix.** Fixing something yourself and telling your manager afterwards is good. Telling your manager first, with a proposal, is usually better.
- **Overthinking ranks 2 and 3.** If both seem possible, use step 4. Real action beats avoidance.
- **Forgetting the stated rule.** If the scenario names a policy, the option that ignores it drops down the order.

## Timing

Ranking takes longer than a single choice. As guidance, allow up to 90 seconds per four-option ranking item in practice, and aim for about a minute by the end of your preparation. The Civil Service Judgement Test is untimed. In the UCAT, ranking items are three-option most-and-least questions inside the 26-minute section, so the [most and least guide](most-and-least-effective.md) applies there.

## Practice items

These 5 items are original, written for these notes. Rank the four options in each item from 1 (most effective) to 4 (least effective). Write your order down, for example "B, D, A, C", before opening the answers.

**Item 1.** You work in a finance team. You realise you made an error on invoices you sent to three clients last week: each was overcharged by a small amount.

- A. Tell your manager, propose correcting the invoices and contacting the clients, and check whether any other invoices are affected.
- B. Correct the invoices in the system and send the corrected copies to the clients, telling your manager afterwards.
- C. Wait to see whether any client notices before doing anything.
- D. Change the records so it looks as though the mistake came from the billing software.

**Item 2.** You are a healthcare assistant on a hospital ward. You find a patient on the floor beside their bed. They are conscious, say they are fine and want to get back into bed.

- A. Help them straight back into bed, as they ask.
- B. Stay with them, keep them still and comfortable, and call for a nurse using the call system.
- C. Leave them where they are and go to find a nurse.
- D. Help them up and say nothing about it, as they said they were fine.

**Item 3.** You are a graduate trainee. In a client meeting, your manager quotes a market statistic that you know is out of date. The newer figure changes the recommendation slightly.

- A. Send the client the correct figure yourself after the meeting, without telling your manager.
- B. Flag it quietly to your manager at a natural pause, or with a discreet note, so they can correct it before the meeting ends.
- C. Raise it with your manager straight after the meeting and offer to send the client the updated figure.
- D. Say nothing, and later tell colleagues that your manager does not know the numbers.

**Item 4.** You are a teaching assistant in a primary school. A pupil tells you something that suggests they are being harmed at home, and asks you to keep it a secret.

- A. Promise to keep the secret, so the pupil keeps trusting you.
- B. Listen, explain kindly that you cannot keep it secret because you need to help keep them safe, and tell the school's safeguarding lead the same day.
- C. Tell the safeguarding lead the same day, without telling the pupil what will happen.
- D. Mention it to the class teacher at the end of the week.

**Item 5.** You are a newly qualified nurse. A doctor has prescribed a dose that looks ten times higher than the usual dose for this drug.

- A. Check the dose against the drug reference guide and contact the prescriber to query it before giving anything.
- B. Give half the prescribed dose as a compromise.
- C. Do not give it, and mention it to the doctor at the next ward round.
- D. Ask the nurse in charge to check it with you, and decide together whether to call the doctor.

<summary>Answers and explanations</summary>

**Item 1: A, B, C, D.**
1. **A** owns the mistake, fixes it, keeps your manager informed before acting, and looks for the wider problem.
2. **B** fixes the invoices properly, but the manager hears late and nobody checks for other errors. *Why not 1:* A does everything B does, plus the earlier conversation and the wider check.
3. **C** does nothing and hopes. Clients stay overcharged. *Why not 4:* it is passive rather than dishonest.
4. **D** falsifies records to hide your mistake. That is dishonest and could cause far bigger problems.

**Item 2: B, C, A, D.**
1. **B** keeps the patient safe where they are and gets a trained person to assess them before they are moved.
2. **C** gets a nurse involved, but leaves the patient alone on the floor. *Why not 1:* the call system lets you get help without leaving them.
3. **A** responds kindly, but moving someone after a fall before they are checked can make an injury worse.
4. **D** moves them and hides the fall, so nobody checks them for injury and the risk of another fall is not looked at.

**Item 3: B, C, A, D.**
1. **B** gets the client the right figure before they leave, and lets your manager correct it in their own words.
2. **C** gets the right figure to the client, a little later, with your manager's knowledge. *Why not 1:* the client leaves with the wrong number for a while.
3. **A** fixes the figure but goes behind your manager's back, which can confuse the client and damage trust in the team.
4. **D** leaves the client with the wrong figure and adds gossip about your manager. It fixes nothing and makes the team look worse.

**Item 4: B, C, D, A.**
1. **B** follows safeguarding practice: listen, be honest that you must pass it on, and report it the same day.
2. **C** gets the concern to the right person promptly, which matters most, but does not prepare the pupil. *Why not 1:* being honest with the pupil helps them trust adults who are trying to help.
3. **D** tells someone, but days later and not the person whose job it is to act.
4. **A** promises secrecy, which leaves the pupil without help and is a promise you cannot keep.

**Item 5: A, D, C, B.**
1. **A** checks the facts and goes straight to the person who can confirm or correct the prescription, before any harm can happen.
2. **D** brings in a senior colleague and a second check. It is safe but slower. *Why not 1:* the prescriber is the person who can actually fix the error.
3. **C** prevents an overdose, but the patient may go without a needed drug until the ward round, and the error is not corrected.
4. **B** gives a dose nobody prescribed. Guessing with medication is dangerous.

## Score yourself

Give yourself one mark for every option you placed in the correct position, so up to 4 marks per item and 20 in total.

| Item | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 | 5 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Order | A B C D | B C A D | B C A D | B C D A | A D C B |

- **18 to 20:** strong. You are separating "helpful" from "harmless" well.
- **14 to 17:** check whether your misses cluster in the middle ranks. If so, use step 4 of the method every time: real action beats avoidance.
- **13 or fewer:** start with rank 1 and rank 4 only for a few days. When those are reliable, the middle usually follows.
