# Rating importance

**In one line:** you rate how much each factor should matter to a person who is making a decision.

This format appears in the UCAT SJT, and some employers use versions of it. It is different from every other SJT format: you are not judging actions at all. You are judging the reasons someone should weigh before they act. People who are strong at the action formats sometimes struggle here at first, because the habit of asking "is this a good thing to do?" does not fit.

## What it measures

Importance items test whether you can see what really matters in a situation and what is noise. Good judgement starts before the action: with knowing which facts should drive the decision and which should not influence it at all.

## How the questions look

A scenario describes a person who has to make a decision. Then a list of considerations. You rate each one on its own. The UCAT scale is:

| Label | Short meaning |
|---|---|
| **Very important** | The decision cannot be right without taking this into account |
| **Important** | This should be weighed, but it does not decide the matter alone |
| **Of minor importance** | Relevant, but it should only tip the balance at the margin |
| **Not important at all** | This should not influence the decision |

As with other rating items, several considerations can share a label. You are rating from the point of view of the person in the scenario.

## The scoring frame used in these notes

For importance items, the five checks used across these notes become a ranking of *what kind* of factor you are looking at:

1. **Very important:** safety of people, honesty, rules that apply, and the core duty of the role (for a student: patient safety, professional rules, and their learning obligations).
2. **Important:** practical factors that shape *how* the person acts, such as the limits of their role, other options open to them, or what they have been asked to do by a supervisor.
3. **Of minor importance:** personal convenience, small costs, relationships, or timing that matters a little but should not override anything above.
4. **Not important at all:** factors that are irrelevant to the decision, or that would be wrong to let influence it, such as someone's mood, a past favour, or how something looks.

## A method that works

1. **Name the decision** in one sentence. "Should Callum share his essay?" Many errors come from rating against the wrong decision.
2. **Find the very important factor first.** Usually one or two considerations involve safety, honesty or a rule. Mark them.
3. **Find what should not count at all.** Look for moods, favours, appearances, and personal details that have nothing to do with doing the right thing.
4. **Place the rest.** Ask: does this change *how* the person should act? If yes, Important. If it only adds a small cost or convenience, Of minor importance.
5. **Check against your gut.** If you would be uncomfortable explaining to a senior colleague that this factor drove your decision, it is probably lower than you think.

## Traps that cost points

- **Rating the action instead of the factor.** The question is not "would this be a good thing to do?" It is "how much should this weigh?"
- **Making everything important.** Most sets have at least one factor that should not count. Find it.
- **Promoting personal factors.** Friendship, a past favour or your own plans are real, but rarely more than minor once safety or rules are involved.
- **Ignoring the stated rule.** If a rule is named in the scenario, the factor about that rule is almost always Very important.
- **Forgetting the point of view.** A student should weigh their limits of competence more heavily than a senior doctor would.

## Timing

In the UCAT, importance items share the 26 minutes for 69 questions, so plan on seconds per rating. As guidance: spend your time naming the decision (step 1). Once that is clear, each rating should take 10 to 15 seconds.

## Practice items

These 16 items are original, written for these notes. Rate each consideration: Very important, Important, Of minor importance, or Not important at all.

### Scenario 1

> Callum is a medical student. A friend in his year asks to see Callum's completed essay for an assessed coursework task "just to see the structure". The course rules say that assessed work must be the student's own.

How important are each of the following for Callum in deciding how to respond?

**Item 1.** The course rule that assessed work must be the student's own.

**Item 2.** That Callum's friend helped him with his lecture notes earlier in the year.

**Item 3.** The mark Callum expects his essay to receive.

**Item 4.** Whether there are other ways Callum could help, such as pointing his friend to the study skills guidance.

<details>
<summary>Answers and explanations, items 1 to 4</summary>

**Item 1: Very important.** Sharing assessed work risks breaking a stated rule, for both students. The decision cannot be right without it.

**Item 2: Of minor importance.** The friendship is real and may affect how kindly he says no, but a past favour should not override the rule. *Why not Not important at all:* it does reasonably affect his tone.

**Item 3: Not important at all.** Whether the essay is good or bad has nothing to do with whether he should share it.

**Item 4: Important.** It shapes how Callum can say no while still supporting his friend. *Why not Very important:* the rule decides the matter. This only affects how he responds.

</details>

### Scenario 2

> Daniel is a medical student on a ward. He notices that an elderly patient he spoke to earlier seems confused and is trying to climb out of bed. The nurses are all busy with other patients.

How important are each of the following for Daniel in deciding what to do?

**Item 5.** That Daniel is not qualified to give the patient treatment.

**Item 6.** That Daniel has not yet been introduced to the ward manager.

**Item 7.** The risk that the patient might fall and hurt themselves.

**Item 8.** Whether the patient's family are due to visit later today.

<details>
<summary>Answers and explanations, items 5 to 8</summary>

**Item 5: Important.** It shapes what Daniel should do: keep the patient safe and get a member of staff, not try to treat or sedate them. *Why not Very important:* it guides his action, but the safety risk is the reason to act at all.

**Item 6: Not important at all.** Whether he has met the ward manager has no bearing on keeping the patient safe. He can call any member of staff.

**Item 7: Very important.** Patient safety is the core of this decision.

**Item 8: Of minor importance.** The family might help settle the patient later, but they are not there now and the risk is now. *Why not Not important at all:* it is slightly relevant to the patient's care over the day.

</details>

### Scenario 3

> Ravi is a graduate trainee. His manager has asked him to recommend a supplier for the office's printing contract. One of the three suppliers bidding is run by Ravi's cousin. The company has a policy on declaring conflicts of interest.

How important are each of the following for Ravi in deciding how to handle the recommendation?

**Item 9.** That his cousin would be disappointed not to win the contract.

**Item 10.** The company's policy on declaring conflicts of interest.

**Item 11.** That his cousin's company has submitted the lowest quote.

**Item 12.** That his manager wants a recommendation by Friday.

<details>
<summary>Answers and explanations, items 9 to 12</summary>

**Item 9: Not important at all.** His cousin's feelings must play no part in a fair business decision.

**Item 10: Very important.** Ravi must declare the conflict before he does anything else, and the policy tells him how.

**Item 11: Important.** Cost is a real part of choosing a supplier, so the quote must be weighed, but openly and probably by someone else given the conflict. *Why not Very important:* it cannot decide how Ravi handles his own conflict. *Why not minor:* price genuinely matters to the company's decision.

**Item 12: Of minor importance.** The deadline affects timing, so he should declare the conflict quickly, but it should not change what he does.

</details>

### Scenario 4

> Megan is a student with a part-time job in a shop. On Monday her shift manager asks her to work an extra evening shift on Thursday. Megan has an important university exam on Friday morning.

How important are each of the following for Megan in deciding whether to accept the shift?

**Item 13.** The extra pay she would earn from the shift.

**Item 14.** Megan's need to be rested and prepared for her exam.

**Item 15.** That her manager seemed to be in a bad mood when asking.

**Item 16.** Whether she could offer to work a different extra shift later in the week instead.

<details>
<summary>Answers and explanations, items 13 to 16</summary>

**Item 13: Of minor importance.** Money matters to most students, but a single shift's pay should not outweigh an exam the next day.

**Item 14: Very important.** Her studies are her main commitment, and the exam is the next morning. This should drive the decision.

**Item 15: Not important at all.** Her manager's mood should not influence whether she takes the shift.

**Item 16: Important.** An alternative lets her help the shop without risking the exam, so it shapes how she responds. *Why not Very important:* it is a way of acting on the decision, not the reason for it.

</details>

## Score yourself

| Item | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 | 5 | 6 | 7 | 8 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Answer | VI | M | N | I | I | N | VI | M |

| Item | 9 | 10 | 11 | 12 | 13 | 14 | 15 | 16 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Answer | N | VI | I | M | M | VI | N | I |

Key: VI = Very important, I = Important, M = Of minor importance, N = Not important at all.

Each practice scenario here happens to use every label once, so you can see the whole scale. Real tests will not do that: several factors can share a label, and some labels may not appear at all. Judge every factor on its own.

- **14 to 16:** you are reading the decision correctly.
- **10 to 13:** check whether you are promoting personal factors (friendship, money, convenience) too high. Reread trap 3.
- **9 or fewer:** go back to step 1 and write the decision down before rating anything.
